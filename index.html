The Hedonic Primacy Project

Research on consciousness, adaptation,
and the engineering of experience

Thomas S. Peacock

This site hosts three pieces of research on hedonic primacy — the philosophical position that positive hedonic experience is the only thing that is intrinsically good, and that the central project of applied ethics is the engineering of conscious experience rather than the management of its symptoms. The first paper defends the position and its implications across ethics, philosophy of mind, and personal identity. The second, a philosophical sequel, proves the position by exhaustive reduction — showing case by case that nothing we call good or bad is separate from feeling — and follows its consequences to their furthest point. The third investigates the neuroscience of hedonic adaptation and outlines experimental paths toward substrate-level intervention. All three are preliminary and open to revision. Feedback is welcome.

Paper One · Philosophy

The Case for Hedonic Primacy

A Framework for Consciousness, Adaptation, and the Engineering of Experience

A systematic defense of hedonic primacy integrated with consciousness-centric ethics, open individualism, gradual substrate replacement, and a two-level structure distinguishing endpoint ideals from transitional rules. The paper's central epistemological contribution is the phenomenological asymmetry argument: peak hedonic states contain evidence that sober reflection systematically underweights, which means standard philosophical adjudication of hedonic questions rests on an incomplete evidentiary base. The paper is presented as revisable in principle, but holds its core claims — that positive valence is the only intrinsic good, that consciousness rather than species membership is the morally relevant property, and that the engineering of positive experience is the obligatory project of a civilisation that has understood itself — with as much confidence as any ethical position the author has been able to construct.

Abstract

This paper sets out a philosophical framework I have arrived at through a combination of first-person experience with altered hedonic states, engagement with the neuroscience of reward and adaptation, and extended dialogue with the existing literature on ethics and personal identity. The framework rests on a single foundational claim: positive valence is the only thing that is intrinsically good, and negative valence is the only thing that is intrinsically bad. Every secondary value that humans pursue — meaning, purpose, dignity, accomplishment, freedom, authenticity, even love — derives its value from the hedonic experiences it produces, and nothing else. I argue that the apparent disagreement with this claim in both common intuition and academic philosophy is not evidence against it, but rather an artifact of two related problems: biological hedonic adaptation, which prevents sustained positive experience and therefore conditions us to treat wellbeing as a scarce luxury rather than the point of existence, and the phenomenological asymmetry between peak hedonic states and sober reflection, which means that the sober mind cannot fully access the evidence on which hedonic primacy rests. From the foundational claim I derive a consciousness-centric rather than species-centric ethics, an intensity-threshold account of suffering, a two-level structure distinguishing endpoint ideals from transitional rules, and an account of personal identity grounded in open individualism and gradual substrate replacement that permits cosmic-scale hedonic engineering without requiring death of the self. I close by stating the precautionary conditions under which that engineering would be permissible, and the conditions under which it would not. The framework is presented as revisable in principle. Its core, however, I hold to be as close to self-evidently true as any ethical claim has ever been, for reasons the paper tries to make visible to the reader who has not yet felt them.

Paper Two · Philosophy

The Hidden Valence

Why Nothing We Call Good Or Bad Is Separate From Feeling

The philosophical sequel to The Case for Hedonic Primacy. Where the first paper asserted that positive valence is the only intrinsic good and negative valence the only intrinsic bad, this one sets out to prove it by exhaustive reduction. It introduces the subtraction test — hold any supposed good fully present, remove only the feeling, and ask whether anyone would still want what is left — and runs that test in the open on the hardest cases there are: money, status, love, truth, meaning, achievement, beauty, justice, freedom, and life itself. None survives. The paper then explains why, if this is true, almost no one sees it; grounds the argument in the documented neuroscience of reward and adaptation; and follows the consequences into the identity of the being who would benefit from a hedonic future, the population ethics of how much experience there should be, the universal language of valence that drains conflict of its fuel and renders freedom eventually unnecessary, and the single empirical question on which everything finally waits — what valence physically is. Like its predecessor, it is offered as revisable in principle while holding its core as firmly as any ethical claim can be held.

Abstract

In an earlier paper, The Case for Hedonic Primacy, I argued that positive valence is the only thing that is intrinsically good, and negative valence the only thing that is intrinsically bad. That paper stated the claim and traced its consequences. What it never did was the one thing that would make the claim impossible to escape. It did not take the things we believe to be valuable in their own right, one at a time — money, status, love, truth, meaning, achievement, beauty, justice, freedom, health, power, legacy, and life itself — and show of each that the value we find in it is feeling, and nothing but feeling, wearing a disguise. That demonstration is the work of this paper. The instrument is a single test, which I call the subtraction test, and it can be run on any candidate good. Hold the thing fully present, remove only the feeling, and ask whether anyone would still want what is left. I run the test in the open, on the hardest cases I can find, and watch them fall one after another. None survives. I then owe an account of why, if this is so, almost no one sees it, and I give one; a truth denied by nearly everyone needs its denial explained rather than merely deplored. The illusion that some values stand apart from feeling is not stupidity. It is the predictable joint product of four things: an evolved nervous system that hides its own machinery, a world in which a good and the feeling it brings almost never come apart, a language built to name shared objects rather than the private subject who feels them, and a calm reflective stance that lacks access to the very evidence in question. With the reduction in hand, I ground it in the documented neuroscience of reward and adaptation, naming the specific physical mechanisms by which the brain cancels sustained pleasure, and I argue that because they are mechanisms they can be edited. From there the consequences run further than the first paper carried them: into the identity of the being who would benefit from a hedonic future, into the population ethics of how much experience there should be and for whom, into the role freedom keeps after suffering is gone, indispensable yet temporary, lasting only until valence itself is fully understood, into a frankly-labelled speculation about the possible scale of experience, and at last into the single empirical question on which all the rest waits, which is what valence physically is. I hold the central claim to be true. Not as a hypothesis awaiting confirmation, but as the floor every other value has been standing on the whole time, without once looking down.

Paper Three · Neuroscience

Hedonic Technology

The Neuroscience of Adaptation and the Computational Signatures of Peak Experience

An empirical companion to the philosophy paper. It covers the multi-layer architecture of hedonic adaptation at the receptor, transcriptional, and network levels; a computational investigation of reward circuitry in the FlyWire Drosophila connectome; a brain encoding analysis using Meta's TRIBE v2 model; and a set of proposed experimental interventions targeting the mechanisms that prevent sustained positive valence in biological consciousness. The paper is preliminary work and used AI-assisted literature review and analysis during its production. It should be read as empirical groundwork accompanying the philosophy paper rather than as a finished scientific contribution. Individual findings may require independent verification.
